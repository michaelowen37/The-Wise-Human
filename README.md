# The Wise Human

The Wise Human is an adaptive learning platform with a twist. Students master one module
before the next one unlocks. Old material comes back at widening intervals, so it
sticks. And between lessons, a "Wonder" question invites them to think about
something with no right answer at all.

It is built for homeschooling families and microschools, because we share a
philosophy: less seat time, more real life. When the academic work gets done in a
couple of focused hours, the rest of the day opens up. That is why there is a whole
section on practical life skills, from tying shoes to reading a payslip, written for
the educator rather than the child.

## How it works

**Gated modules.** Every module has to be mastered before the one after it opens.
Today, mastery means answering enough questions correctly in a single round, and the
number depends on the subject and the grade. Modules can require others from any
course or grade, so the whole library is one map of what depends on what. Grade 3
multiplication, for instance, requires kindergarten joining and taking away, and a
grade 3 student who never mastered it finds it locked until they do. A prerequisite
from another course gates a module only when that course is assigned, because placing
a student above a grade is the educator's call.

Pre-K courses are the one exception to the chain. Their activities are parallel,
because a three-year-old need not finish patterns before counting to three, so those
courses are marked non-linear and only the prerequisites they name apply.

**Loop-back.** Two failed rounds in a row send a student back to the prerequisite,
even one we thought was solid, for a quick check. Usually the gap is further back
than the module they are stuck on. The educator's report says when it happened.

**Questions that cannot be wrong.** Every question is built from checked rules and
word lists, never composed by a language model at question time. In math the rule is
arithmetic. In reading it is a word list with the facts attached: which words rhyme,
how many syllables each has, which sound each letter makes. An independent test
re-derives every answer from those facts across hundreds of random seeds, so a wrong
answer cannot ship. Each student gets fresh questions every round.

Where this stops is open writing. An essay cannot be generated or judged by a rule.
For older grades the platform can build a prompt from a template and check its
structure, but judging quality needs a person, or an AI acting as an assistant with a
teacher confirming. We say so rather than pretend otherwise.

**Spaced repetition, across courses.** Once a module is mastered, its questions
reappear inside later practice rounds as memory checks, drawn from any course the
student has worked in, not only the one they are in now. Those never count against
the current module. They tell us, and the educator, whether the learning held, and the
report and the transcript both say how the old material fared when it came back.

**Mastery takes two days.** One passing round shows a student can do something
today. It does not show they can do it in three weeks, or inside a bigger problem. So a
passing round opens the next module at once, and the star, mastery, takes a second pass
on a different day. The result screen says which one just happened, the overview shows a
hollow star for passed once, and the report and transcript say "passed once" until the
second day comes. Stars earned before this rule stay earned. Every round carries two memory checks from two different passed modules.

**Placement.** A reader who already knows the early material does not start at the
bottom. The overview offers a placement check in each subject: four questions per grade,
three right to clear it, walking up the band and stopping at the first grade not cleared.
Every module in the cleared grades is unlocked, marked "placed past" rather than mastered,
and the student starts at the grade where the walk stopped.

**Checkpoints.** After every fourth module a student passes, the overview offers a
checkpoint: eight questions drawn from everything they have passed, with nothing new in
it. A miss never removes a pass or a star. It lowers that module's confidence score and
marks the module "missed in the last checkpoint" on the course list and the report, and
passing the module again clears the mark. Checkpoints are their own events, so the
transcript and the who-needs-help list see them.

**Confidence, not just correctness.** Each module carries a score from 1 to 5 built
from mastery, streaks, timing and how the material fares when it comes back. Two
wrong answers in under four seconds earns a quiet "guessing?" on the report, because
tapping fast usually means not thinking.

**Wonder questions.** After every second finished practice round, whether it went
well or not, a student spends a minute on an open question with no right answer, then
reads how four different people might see it: a scientist, an artist, a grandparent
of faith, and a skeptic. None of them is presented as right. Every question is
written toward one of four aims: curiosity about the world; failure as the way
learning happens; naming and handling feelings; and the truth that hard days are a
normal part of being human and that people get through them. The questions rotate
so a student never meets the same one a third time, and after a round that did not
go well the questions about failure and feelings come first, because that is when
they land. For the youngest children there is one spoken question and two short
spoken voices. Nothing a student writes or taps is ever saved. Nothing reaches a
student until someone at their school has read it and approved it, and the pool
grows with the curriculum: a check in the codebase notes any age group with fewer
questions than modules.

**Life skills.** Forty-five so far, in one sequence from earliest to most mature, each
with why it matters and how to practice it. Educators mark them as covered for the
classroom. Nothing here is graded, tracked or shown to a child.

**For children who cannot read yet.** Kindergarten courses are built to work without
words: lessons are spoken one line at a time with the picture doing the work, status
is a star, a ring or a padlock, a wrong answer means try again rather than move on,
and the result screen is a row of stars and one big arrow. Young students sign in by
picture, one of twelve animals on six colours, drawn in the app.

**States.** Most states build to the Common Core; Texas builds to its own standards.
An educator picks their state once, and every course is mapped to both, so the
report shows coverage against the standards that apply to them.

## Privacy, and where the data lives

We collect a school-issued ID, which courses are assigned, and what happened during
practice. That is the whole list. No names, no emails, no photographs, no free text
from children, no advertising, no trackers. Sign-in pictures for young children are
drawn in the app from a fixed set of animals. Read-aloud is output only. There is no
microphone.

There is no server. Everything lives on the device in front of the student. That is a
deliberate choice, not a gap: no child's record sits anywhere we would have to guard.

**Backups.** One file holds the whole classroom: every student, their full history,
and the educator's settings. The educator downloads it, or on an iPad shares it
straight to their email. Restoring it on any device brings everything back, and a
restore can only ever add, never remove. File names say which device made them and
how many students they hold. A backup file also goes to the device's downloads folder
by itself each time a student finishes a course. A backup made today is kept in
the test suite and must restore on every future version.

**Updates.** Because there is no account, an educator gets the newest version simply
by opening the site. Nothing to install and nothing to send.

**One device at a time.** A student's live record lives on the device in front of
them. Signing several devices into one browser account does not share it, because
browsers sync settings and bookmarks, not a website's stored data. Use one device per
student, or restore the classroom backup onto a device before using it. Every finished
course saves a backup file to the device's downloads folder by itself; only the newest one is
needed, and older ones can be deleted at any time. An educator can also download a backup
to a folder of their choosing from the Backup page.

Every number on a report is recomputed from the record of what happened. Nothing is
stored as a score, so a figure can never disagree with the record. It is the record,
counted.

## On copying questions into an AI

You cannot stop a determined teenager pasting a question into a phone, and we do not
pretend to. What we do is make it pointless and make it visible.

- Practice is not graded. Cheating on practice only cheats the student out of
  learning, and the next round brings different numbers anyway.
- Placement and progress checks, the ones that matter, are done with an educator
  present.
- Every question carries fresh numbers for that student and that round, so answers
  cannot be shared.
- Timing is watched. An answer that arrives faster than the question could be read
  is flagged on the report.
- Old material returns weeks later without warning. A student who did not learn it
  the first time cannot fake it the second.
- For older grades, questions increasingly ask for a step, an ordering, a drag or a
  drawing rather than a typed final answer. Those do not paste.

We deliberately do not hide tricks in the text to trip up AI tools. That is a
deception aimed at your own students, and the tools get better at ignoring it every
month.

## Where the material comes from

Every module maps to published standards, and that map is a test in the codebase
that fails while any standard in a grade marked ready has no module. The frameworks:

- **Texas**: the Texas Essential Knowledge and Skills (TEKS) for kindergarten to 12,
  and the Texas Prekindergarten Guidelines for pre-K.
- **Every other state and the District of Columbia**: the Common Core State Standards
  for kindergarten to 12, which most states adopted and some lightly revised. For
  pre-K, where there is no Common Core, the federal Head Start Early Learning
  Outcomes Framework, which daycare centers nationwide already use.

An educator picks their state once, and reports show coverage against the framework
that applies to them.

## The methods, and the evidence behind them

None of these ideas is ours. Each has decades of research behind it, and the
platform's job is to apply them consistently.

- **Mastery learning.** Students do not move on until they have shown they can do the
  thing. Bloom, "Learning for Mastery" (1968), and "The 2 Sigma Problem" (Educational
  Researcher, 1984). Kulik, Kulik and Bangert-Drowns, meta-analysis of mastery
  learning programs (Review of Educational Research, 1990).
- **Retrieval practice.** Answering questions strengthens memory far more than
  re-reading. Roediger and Karpicke, "Test-Enhanced Learning" (Psychological Science,
  2006). Karpicke and Blunt (Science, 2011).
- **Spaced repetition.** Material returns at widening intervals. Ebbinghaus (1885).
  Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted and Rohrer, meta-analysis of distributed practice
  (Psychological Bulletin, 2006). Dunlosky and colleagues, "Improving Students'
  Learning With Effective Learning Techniques" (Psychological Science in the Public
  Interest, 2013), which rates spaced practice and practice testing as the two most
  effective techniques studied.
- **Immediate, specific feedback.** Every answer is explained straight away. Hattie
  and Timperley, "The Power of Feedback" (Review of Educational Research, 2007).
- **Worked examples first.** Lessons for young children show the example before the
  rule. Sweller's cognitive load theory and the worked-example effect: Sweller, van
  Merrienboer and Paas (Educational Psychology Review, 1998).
- **Systematic phonics for early reading.** Letter sounds, blending, rhymes and
  syllables in that order. The National Reading Panel report (2000).

These citations were written from memory and should be checked against the papers
before they appear in anything a school reads. What we do not claim is that students
outperform anyone. That is a result to be earned with data from real classrooms.

## Devices

The Wise Human runs in any modern browser. For pre-K and kindergarten, an iPad or another large
tablet with a stylus is the right device: the youngest modules are tracing, connect-the-dots
and first strokes, and they require a touch screen. They refuse a mouse on purpose, because the
point of those modules is practice holding a stylus. Readers can use anything.

## Running it

**To use it:** open `index.html`. That one file is the whole product, with React
inside it, so it works from GitHub Pages, from a school's own web server, from a USB
stick, or double-clicked from a folder on a laptop. Nothing is fetched from anywhere.

**To publish it:** push this repository to GitHub, turn on GitHub Pages for the main
branch in the repository settings, and the app is live at
`https://<your-name>.github.io/<repository>/`. Every push updates it. Educators get the
newest version by opening the address.

**To work on it:**
- `./check.sh` runs everything: the rules tests, curriculum coverage, old-backup
  restore, the build, a render check, a syntax check, a real-browser click-through of
  every screen, and a reload test of the standalone page. It must end with
  `ALL CHECKS PASSED`.
- `node tests/logic.test.mjs` runs the rules alone, which is the fastest loop when
  changing content or logic.
- `./build.sh` generates two things from `src/logic.mjs` (content and rules),
  `src/curriculum.mjs` (the standards map) and `src/ui.jsx` (screens):
  `dist/edusphere-prototype.jsx` for the chat preview, and `index.html` for everyone
  else. Edit the sources, never the generated files.
- Building needs Node, TypeScript, and `react` and `react-dom` installed globally
  (`npm install -g typescript react react-dom`), plus `playwright` for the browser
  tests.

Read `CLAUDE.md` before changing anything. Decisions and the phase 1 plan are in
`docs/DECISIONS.md`. Design rules are in `docs/DESIGN.md`. What to do when something
breaks is in `docs/RUNBOOK.md`.

## Where it stands

One file, no server, no accounts, nothing leaves the device. Sixty-two modules:
pre-K first steps (four), kindergarten counting (eleven) and letters (nine), grade 1
numbers to 20 (five) and reading (five), grade 2 numbers to 1,000 (six) and reading
(four), grade 3 fractions (five), multiplication and division (four) and reading
(four), and grade 4 bigger numbers and operations (five). Every grade and subject
that exists, pre-K through grade 3 in reading and pre-K through grade 4 in math, is
complete against its frameworks and marked ready, so any future gap fails the checks. The map in
`src/curriculum.mjs` is the work list for everything else.

Next: letter tracing with a finger, the spoken two-voice reflection for the youngest
children, placement checks, a class-wide report ranking who needs help most, and
content grade by grade. Accounts and a database are deferred until a customer needs
several devices to share one view, and will not begin before an education privacy
attorney has reviewed the plan.

If you are a developer, or a model, picking this up cold: `CLAUDE.md` lists the files
to read and the order to read them in.
